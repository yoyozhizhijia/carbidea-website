How to Order Custom Tungsten Carbide Parts: From Drawing to Finished Part

Ordering a custom tungsten carbide part is a five-step chain — drawing, grade selection, sintering, grinding, and inspection — and almost all rework cost happens in the first two steps, not the last two. A complete drawing with tolerance and surface requirements, plus the real service conditions (pressure drop, temperature, media, impact), is worth more than any grade-name shortcut. Match the grade family to the wear mechanism instead of naming a specific grade, understand that sintering builds the material while grinding builds the dimension, and ask for per-batch inspection reports at the end. Done in that order, a custom carbide order at prototype or low volume becomes a predictable, repeatable process instead of a negotiation.

Step 1 — Send a complete drawing

The single highest-leverage document in the whole order is the drawing. For carbide, three things matter more than anything else:

  1. Tolerance loops on functional surfaces only. Mark the surfaces that actually mate or seal. On a valve seat, that is the seating diameter and the face; on a bushing, the bore and the OD. If a tolerance is not functionally necessary, leave it at a commercial tolerance — tight grinding is where carbide cost multiplies, often by an order of magnitude from ±0.004 in to ±0.0005 in.
  2. Surface finish and edge condition. Specify Ra values and whether edges are to be chamfered, broken, or sharp. EDM or ground? Polished sealing faces behave differently against soft seats.
  3. Units and datum. State inches or mm explicitly, and give datums. A mixed-unit drawing is the most common cause of a wrong first article.

Step 2 — Choose a grade family, not a grade name

Buyers often ask for a specific carbide grade by name. In custom low-volume manufacturing, the more reliable question is: what is the service, and which grade family fits it?

  • High-toughness class (higher binder, coarser grain) for impact, chipping, and interrupted loads — valve trim, downhole tools, stamping dies.
  • Low-cobalt fine-grain class for cutting-type erosion and fine abrasion — high-velocity flow control, small orifices.
  • Nickel-binder / corrosion-resistant class where the media attacks the binder — chemical service, some slurry chemistries.
  • High-temperature class where binder softening matters — steam or hot-gas service.

Give the supplier the operating data: media, temperature, pressure drop, particle size and velocity if applicable, and any history photo of the failed part. With that, a competent supplier recommends the family and confirms it against the drawing — that is the conversation to insist on.

Step 3 — Sintering: where the material is made

Sintering converts the pressed body into dense carbide. Two things to know:

  • Near-net shape. Parts are pressed or formed close to final shape, so the sintered body is oversized only where grinding will remove material. Complex pockets and through-holes are best designed in at this stage.
  • HIP (hot isostatic pressing). If the application cannot tolerate internal porosity — high-pressure sealing or highly stressed sections — ask whether HIP is included or available. It removes the last residual porosity and is a standard, verifiable step, not a mystery process.

Ask for the sintered density per batch; it is the quickest single number that confirms the material was made correctly.

Step 4 — Grinding and finishing: what is realistic

Grinding turns the sintered body into a finished part. Realistic expectations:

  • Ground OD/ID and faces: tolerances to about ±0.0005 in (0.013 mm) are achievable on controlled features; ±0.001–0.002 in is a comfortable commercial range.
  • Flatness and parallelism on sealing faces: commonly held to 0.0002–0.0004 in with proper setup.
  • Un-ground as-sintered surfaces: expect 0.004–0.008 in variation — do not put tight tolerances on as-sintered areas.

If your drawing mixes as-sintered and ground zones, say so; it changes how the supplier plans the sintering oversize.

Step 5 — Inspection and delivery

For prototypes and low-volume production, agree the verification package up front:

  • Per-batch density and hardness test reports.
  • Dimensional report on functional surfaces (the tolerance loops from Step 1).
  • Material certificate if your customer or spec requires traceability.

For low-volume NPI runs, the whole inspection can be finished and documented in days, not weeks — the point is that it is agreed before you order, not discovered after.

The checklist before you send a drawing

  1. Drawing with tolerance loops, surface finish, datum, and units (inches or mm).
  2. Service conditions — media, temperature, pressure drop, impact or erosion data.
  3. Quantities and delivery target; note whether this is a prototype or production run.
  4. Acceptance criteria — density, hardness, dimensional report, certificate needs.
  5. Special requirements — HIP, coating, marking, packaging — stated explicitly.

FAQ

Q: Do I need to specify a carbide grade, or can the supplier choose?
A: Both — but specify the service, and let a competent supplier confirm the grade family against it. Naming only a grade without service conditions is how mismatches happen; naming only service conditions leaves the material unvalidated.

Q: What does HIP do that normal sintering does not?
A: HIP applies heat and pressure to close residual porosity in the sintered body. For pressure-sealing or highly stressed carbide parts it removes a failure mode that grinding cannot fix later.

Q: How tight a tolerance can custom carbide parts be ground to?
A: Around ±0.0005 in (0.013 mm) on controlled functional features with commercial feasibility; tighter than that is possible on specific features but costs disproportionately.

Q: Why does a custom carbide part cost more than a standard blank?
A: Because every step — sintering near-net shape, grinding to print, inspection per batch — is configured for one drawing. The cost is in the engineering and setup, which is why the drawing quality and clear service data are the real levers.

Q: How long does a prototype carbide order take?
A: It depends on the size and complexity of the part, but a prototype from an approved drawing typically runs in weeks, not months — the critical path is usually the drawing and grade confirmation, not the machining.
